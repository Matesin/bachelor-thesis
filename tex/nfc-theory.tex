\chap[nfc-theory] NFC

This chapter introduces the principles of the {\sbf Near Field Communication (NFC)} technology. Due to the extensive nature of NFC standardization, the {\sbf main focus} is placed on those of its {\sbf mechanisms} that are {\sbf relevant} to the presented {\sbf implementation}. 

\nl Other technologies within the NFC ecosystem are discussed only briefly to provide context and completeness.

\sec[nfc-intro] Introduction to NFC

\nl Near Field Communication (NFC) is a short-range wireless communication technology derived from radio-frequency identification (RFID) systems. It enables bidirectional data exchange between devices in close proximity, typically within a range of up to 10~cm, with reliable operation occurring at distances of approximately 4~cm~\cite[rfid-nfc-introduction].

\nl NFC operates at a carrier frequency of 13.56~MHz and is based on inductive coupling between loop antennas. In this mechanism, an active device generates an electromagnetic field, while a passive device modulates this field to transmit data.

\nl The NFC technology is standardized by international organizations, primarily ISO/IEC and the NFC Forum\urlnote{https://nfc-forum.org}. The key specifications include ISO/IEC~14443, which defines proximity card communication (used in NFC-A and NFC-B), and ISO/IEC~18092, which specifies peer-to-peer communication. In addition, the NFC Forum defines higher-level protocols and data formats to ensure interoperability across devices.

\nl NFC supports three primary operating modes: reader/writer mode, card emulation mode, and peer-to-peer mode. From the perspective of this thesis, card emulation mode is of particular importance, as it allows the device to behave as a contactless smart card and participate in ISO-DEP communication with an external reader.

\sec[nfc-device-types] Device Types

NFC devices can be broadly categorized into {\sbf active} and {\sbf passive} types. 

\nl {\sbf Active devices}, often referred to as {\sbf universal NFC frontends}, are equipped with their own power source and are capable of generating an electromagnetic field. This enables them to initiate communication and control the timing and parameters of the exchange.

\nl In contrast, {\sbf passive devices} (commonly implemented as {\sbf dynamic} NFC tags) do not require an internal power supply. Instead, they rely on the energy harvested from the electromagnetic field generated by an active device (Energy Harvesting). Communication from the passive side is achieved through load modulation, i.e., by varying the impedance of the antenna circuit, which in turn modulates the carrier field.

\sec[nfc-network-hierarchy] Network Hierarchy

In NFC communication, two roles are distinguished according to which device initiates the interaction: the \textit{initiator} and the \textit{target}. These roles are defined at the protocol level and are independent of the specific hardware capabilities of the devices.

\begitems
* {\sbf Initiator}
\nl The device that actively starts the communication and generates the initial RF field. It is responsible for polling and controlling the communication sequence, as is typically the case for an NFC reader.

* {\sbf Target}
\nl The device that responds to the initiator. Depending on the communication mode, it can passively modulate the initiator’s field or actively generate its own signal. Typical examples include contactless smart cards, NFC tags, or peer devices.
\enditems

\nl It is important to distinguish these roles from the physical ability to generate an electromagnetic field. Although the initiator is typically the field-generating device, this is not strictly required in all communication modes.

\nl NFC defines two communication modes based on how the electromagnetic field is generated and how the devices are powered: \textit{active mode} and \textit{passive mode}.

\begitems

* {\sbf Active Mode}
\nl In active mode, both the initiator and the target are equipped with their own power sources and are capable of generating an RF field. Communication is performed through alternating transmission, where each device generates its own carrier field during its transmission phase. This mode is used primarily in peer-to-peer communication scenarios.

* {\sbf Passive Mode}
\nl In passive mode, only the initiator generates the RF field. The target does not have its own power source and utilizes energy harvesting. Data transmission from the target is achieved through load modulation of the existing carrier field. This mode is characteristic of typical NFC-A and NFC-B interactions, such as communication with contactless smart cards and NFC tags.
\enditems

\sec [nfc-operating-modes] Operating Modes

The NFC Forum defines three primary operating modes that determine the role and behavior of a device within a communication session.

\begitems

* {\sbf Reader/Writer Mode}
\nl In the reader/writer mode, the NFC device operates as an initiator and interacts with passive NFC tags. The device generates an electromagnetic field and communicates with tags compliant with standards such as ISO/IEC 14443 (Type 2 and Type 4 Tags) or ISO/IEC 15693 (Type 5 Tags). This mode is commonly used for applications such as reading NDEF records, accessing contactless cards, or configuring embedded devices. 

* {\sbf Card Emulation Mode}
\nl In card emulation (CE) mode, the NFC device behaves as a contactless smart card and acts as a target. It responds to commands issued by an external reader, typically using ISO/IEC 14443 protocols. This mode is widely used in applications such as contactless payments, access control, and mobile wallets. This mode has two variants of implementation. It can either be implemented on a general-purpose MCU or using a dedicated secure element (SE) hardware, depending on the use case. The majority of security keys supporting NFC uses the SE variant (as implied, for example, by the Yubikey tech manual~\cite[yubikey-manual] or by the Google Titan documentation~\cite[titan-key-documentation]).

* {\sbf Peer-to-Peer Mode} 
\nl The peer-to-peer mode enables half-duplex communication between two NFC-enabled devices, where both devices can alternately assume the roles of initiator and target. Communication in this mode is defined by ISO/IEC 18092 and the NFC Forum Logical Link Control Protocol (LLCP). It is primarily used for data exchange scenarios such as device pairing or file transfer, although its practical use has declined in favor of higher-throughput wireless technologies.

\enditems

\nl These operating modes build upon the previously described concepts of initiator and target roles, as well as active and passive communication modes. Their combination defines the functional behavior of NFC devices in real-world applications.

\sec[nfc-tag-types] Types of NFC Tags
\secc[nfc-a] NFC-A

NFC-A is based on the ISO/IEC 14443 Type A specification. Data transmission in this standard employs Miller (delay) encoding in combination with Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) modulation, with a typical data rate of 106 kbit/s.

\nl Binary information is conveyed by variations in the amplitude of the carrier signal. Specifically, logical values are not represented by absolute voltage levels, but rather by the presence or absence of modulation and by transitions between minimum and maximum signal amplitude. In Miller encoding, these transitions are time-dependent, meaning that the information is encoded both in the timing and structure of the signal changes, which improves robustness against noise and enables reliable synchronization between communicating devices.

\secc[nfc-b] NFC-B

NFC-B is based on the ISO/IEC 14443 Type B specification. It uses NRZ-L (Non-Return-to-Zero Level) encoding for data representation, combined with Binary Phase Shift Keying (BPSK) for subcarrier modulation during card-to-reader transmission. For reader-to-card transmission, a 10\% ASK modulation is applied directly to the carrier, where a reduction in carrier amplitude to approximately 90\% of its nominal level represents a logical 0, while the full amplitude represents a logical 1.

\nl Due to the lower modulation index compared to NFC-A, the carrier signal remains more stable, resulting in improved robustness against electromagnetic interference. NFC-B is used primarily in electronic identification documents such as electronic passports, where enhanced reliability and support for more complex security mechanisms are required~\cite[conrad-nfc-specs].

\secc[nfc-f] NFC-F

NFC-F is based on the JIS X 6319-4 specification and was originally developed by Sony under the commercial name FeliCa. Unlike NFC-A and NFC-B, NFC-F is not strictly derived from the ISO/IEC 14443 family, but instead follows a different protocol structure defined in Japanese industrial standards.

\nl A key distinguishing feature of NFC-F is its higher data rates, typically 212 kbit/s or 424 kbit/s, which makes it suitable for latency-sensitive applications. The technology is widely deployed in Japan, particularly in transportation systems, contactless payment infrastructures, and access control solutions. Its design emphasizes fast transaction times and efficient multi-service integration.

\nl However, due to its regional focus and protocol differences, NFC-F is not universally supported by all NFC readers. Consequently, its applicability in general-purpose NFC implementations is limited.

\sec[nfc-protocols] NFC Communication Protocols

NFC communication is built upon a layered protocol stack that defines how data is exchanged between devices after the initial activation phase. At the transport and higher communication layers, two primary protocols are used: ISO-DEP and NFC-DEP. In addition, several auxiliary protocol layers defined by the NFC Forum provide standardized data exchange formats.

\secc[nfc-iso-dep] ISO-DEP

The {\sbf ISO-DEP} (ISO Data Exchange Protocol), specified in ISO/IEC~14443-4~\cite[iso14443-4], defines a transport layer for reliable data exchange between an NFC initiator and a proximity integrated circuit card. It extends the lower NFC-A/B protocols by introducing a block-oriented, half-duplex communication mechanism that enables structured and robust transmission of application data.

\seccc[nfc-iso-dep-activation] Activation and Parameter Negotiation

\nl Before data exchange can begin, ISO-DEP requires an activation phase in which the initiator selects the target and negotiates communication parameters. This is performed using the RATS (Request for Answer To Select) and ATS (Answer To Select) mechanism.

\nl First, the reader sends a RATS command. The RATS consists of a command byte and a parameter byte, where the upper nibble encodes the Frame Size for the Device Input (FSDI), defining the maximum frame size supported by the reader. The lower nibble may contain an optional Card Identifier (CID), which is used for logical addressing during communication.

\nl The ATS response consists of a total length value (TL), format byte (T0), optional interface bytes (TA, TB, TC) and optional Historical Bytes (up to 15 bytes). These fields define key communication parameters, including timing constraints and frame sizes.

\nl The interface bytes can contain these values: 

\begitems
* {\sbf Frame Waiting Time (FWT)} 
\nl Specifies the maximum time the initiator waits for a response from the target. It is derived from the Frame Waiting Time Integer (FWI) and directly influences the allowable processing time of the card. Violating this constraint typically results in termination of the NFC session.
* {\sbf Waiting Time Extension (WTX)} 
\nl Allows the target to request additional processing time beyond the configured FWT. This mechanism is essential for operations involving computationally intensive tasks, such as cryptographic processing.
* {\sbf Frame Size (FSC/FSD)}
\nl Defines the maximum payload size of a single frame for the card (FSC) and the device (FSD), determining whether chaining is required.
* {\sbf Frame Guard Time (FGT)} 
\nl Specifies the minimum delay between consecutive frames, ensuring proper synchronization between devices.
* {\sbf Device Identifier (DID)}
\nl Allows logical addressing of devices in more complex communication scenarios.
* {\sbf Historical Bytes (HB)}
\nl Optional data included in the ATS, providing additional information about the capabilities of the card.
\enditems

\nl Correct configuration and handling of these parameters is essential to ensure compatibility with readers and mobile devices. Strict adherence to timing constraints, such as the frame waiting time, is required to prevent premature session termination.

\seccc[nfc-iso-dep-data] Data Exchange

\nl Communication in ISO-DEP is organized into protocol data units (blocks), which are exchanged between the initiator and the target. Three types of blocks are defined: {\sbf I-Blocks} to carry application data, {\sbf R-Blocks} for acknowledgments and retransmission requests, and {\sbf S-Blocks} for control operations such as waiting time extension or protocol deactivation. This structure ensures reliable communication even in the presence of transmission errors.

\seccc[nfc-apdu] APDU

\nl ISO-DEP serves as the transport layer for {\sbf Application Protocol Data Units (APDUs)}, which implement a command–response communication model. APDUs are a standardized data format used in smart card systems for structured information exchange defined by ISO/IEC 7816-4.

\nl In this model, the initiator sends a command APDU (C-APDU), and the target responds with a response APDU (R-APDU). This abstraction allows higher-level protocols, including smart card applications and CTAP over NFC, to operate independently of the underlying transmission mechanism while relying on ISO-DEP for reliable delivery.

\nl A command APDU (C-APDU) consists of a header (CLA, INS, P1, P2) and an optional payload defined by the fields Lc (length of command data) and Le (expected length of response data). The response APDU (R-APDU) may contain response data and is always terminated by a two-byte status word (SW1, SW2), indicating the result of the operation.

\nl APDUs can be encoded in short or extended format. In the short format, the length fields are limited to one byte, allowing payloads of up to 255 bytes. The extended format uses two-byte length fields, enabling payloads of up to 65,535 bytes.

\nl In the short format, the C-APDU consists of a 4-byte header followed by the optional fields Lc, Data (0–255 bytes) and Le, where both Lc and Le are encoded using a single byte.

\seccc[nfc-chaining-difference] Chaining mechanisms

\nl Within ISO-DEP, APDUs are transported using I-Blocks. Since the maximum size of a single ISO-DEP frame is limited by the negotiated frame size, APDUs exceeding this size must be fragmented. This is achieved through {\sbf ISO-DEP chaining}, where a single APDU is split across multiple I-Blocks and reassembled by the receiving side.

\nl In addition to transport-level fragmentation, large responses may also be handled at the application level. In the case of short APDUs, the response length is limited by the value of the Le field. If the response exceeds this limit, it can be delivered in multiple steps using the status word mechanism (e.g., SW1~=~\code{0x61}), where the client issues the GET RESPONSE command to retrieve the remaining data.

\nl The two chaining mechanisms operate at different layers: ISO-DEP chaining ensures reliable transmission of a single APDU over multiple frames, while application-level response handling enables the transfer of data exceeding the limits of the APDU format itself.

\secc[nfc-nfc-dep] NFC-DEP

The {\sbf NFC-DEP} (NFC Data Exchange Protocol), defined in ISO/IEC~18092, is designed for peer-to-peer communication between two {\sbf active} NFC devices. Unlike ISO-DEP, which follows a reader–card model, NFC-DEP enables symmetric communication, allowing both devices to alternate between initiator and target roles.

\nl In contrast to ISO-DEP, NFC-DEP does not utilize Application Protocol Data Units (APDUs) and does not follow a strict command–response model. Instead, communication is based on message-oriented data exchange, making it more suitable for peer-to-peer scenarios rather than smart card emulation.

\nl For this reason, NFC-DEP is not applicable to the desired goal of this thesis, as FIDO2 authentication over NFC relies on APDU-based communication provided by ISO-DEP.
