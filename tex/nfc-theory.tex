\chap[nfc-theory] NFC

This chapter introduces the principles of the {\sbf Near Field Communication (NFC)} technology. Due to the extensive nature of NFC standardization, the {\sbf main focus} is placed on those of its {\sbf mechanisms} that are {\sbf relevant} to the presented {\sbf implementation}, specifically {\sbf NFC Type A tags} and the {\sbf ISO-DEP protocol}.

\nl Other technologies within the NFC ecosystem are discussed only briefly to provide context and completeness.

\sec[nfc-intro] Introduction to NFC

{\sbf Near Field Communication (NFC)} is a short-range wireless communication technology derived from radio-frequency identification (RFID) systems. It enables {\sbf bidirectional data exchange} between devices in immediate or close proximity, typically within a range of up to 10~cm, with reliable operation occurring at distances of approximately 4~cm~\cite[rfid-nfc-introduction].

\nl NFC operates at a {\sbf carrier frequency (fc) of 13.56~MHz} and is based on {\sbf inductive coupling} between {\sbf loop antennas}. In this mechanism, defined in ISO/IEC 14443-2, an active device generates an electromagnetic field, while a passive device modulates this field to transmit data.

\nl The NFC technology is standardized by international organizations, primarily by joint standards of the  International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), abbreviated as ISO/IEC, and the NFC Forum\urlnote{https://nfc-forum.org}. The key specifications include ISO/IEC~14443, which defines proximity card communication (used in NFC-A and NFC-B), and ISO/IEC~18092, which specifies peer-to-peer communication. In addition, the NFC Forum defines higher-level protocols and data formats to ensure interoperability across devices.

\nl NFC supports three primary operating modes: reader/writer mode, card emulation mode, and peer-to-peer mode. From the perspective of this thesis, card emulation mode is of particular importance, as it allows the device to behave as a contactless smart card and participate in ISO-DEP communication with an external reader.

\sec[nfc-device-types] Device Types

NFC devices fall into three categories: {\sbf active} devices (proximity coupling devices or PCDs), {\sbf passive} devices (proximity integrated circuit cards or PICCs), and {\sbf hybrid} devices (proximity extended devices or PXDs).

\nl {\sbf Active devices}, or proximity coupling devices({\sbf PCDs}), are equipped with their own power source and generate an electromagnetic field, enabling them to initiate and control NFC communication.

\nl In contrast, {\sbf passive devices} (proximity integrated circuit cards {\sbf PICCs}), commonly implemented as {\sbf dynamic NFC tags}, do {\sbf not require an internal power supply}. Instead, they rely on the energy harvested from the electromagnetic field generated by an active device {\sbf (Energy Harvesting)}. {\sbf Communication} from the passive side is achieved through {\sbf load modulation}, i.e., by varying the impedance of the antenna circuit, which in turn modulates the carrier field. The characteristics of PICCs are defined in ISO/IEC 14443-1. 

\nl {\sbf Hybrid devices}, or proximity extended devices ({\sbf PXDs}), can operate in either PCD or PICC mode. Most toggle modes {\sbf automatically} based on context, though some allow {\sbf manual selection} by the user.

\sec[nfc-network-hierarchy] Network Hierarchy

In NFC communication, two roles are distinguished according to which device initiates the interaction: the {\sbf initiator} and the {\sbf target}. These roles are defined at the protocol level and are independent of the specific hardware capabilities of the devices.

\begitems
* {\sbf Initiator}
\nl The device that actively starts the communication and generates the initial RF field. It is responsible for polling and controlling the communication sequence, as is typically the case for an NFC reader.

* {\sbf Target}
\nl The device that responds to the initiator. Depending on the communication mode, it can passively modulate the initiator’s field or actively generate its own signal. Typical examples include contactless smart cards, NFC tags, or peer devices. 
\enditems

It is important to distinguish these roles from the physical ability to generate an electromagnetic field. Although the initiator is typically the field-generating device, this is not strictly required in all communication modes.

\nl NFC defines two communication modes based on how the electromagnetic field is generated and how the devices are powered: {\sbf active mode} and {\sbf passive mode}.

\begitems

* {\sbf Active Mode}
\nl In active mode, both the initiator and the target are equipped with their own power sources and are capable of generating an RF field. Communication is performed through alternating transmission, where each device generates its own carrier field during its transmission phase. This mode is used primarily in peer-to-peer communication scenarios.

* {\sbf Passive Mode}
\nl In passive mode, only the initiator generates the RF field. The target does not have its own power source and utilizes energy harvesting. Data transmission from the target is achieved through load modulation of the existing carrier field. This mode is characteristic of typical NFC-A and NFC-B interactions, such as communication with contactless smart cards and NFC tags.
\enditems

\sec [nfc-operating-modes] Operating Modes

The NFC Forum defines {\sbf three} primary {\sbf operating modes} that determine the role and behavior of a device within a communication session.

\begitems

* {\sbf Reader/Writer Mode}
\nl In the reader/writer mode, the NFC device operates as an initiator and interacts with passive NFC tags. The device generates an electromagnetic field and communicates with tags compliant with standards such as ISO/IEC 14443 (Type 2 and Type 4 Tags) or ISO/IEC 15693 (Type 5 Tags). This mode is commonly used for applications such as reading NDEF records, accessing contactless cards, or configuring embedded devices. 

* {\sbf Card Emulation Mode}
\nl In card emulation (CE) mode, the NFC device behaves as a contactless smart card and acts as a target. It responds to commands issued by an external reader, typically using ISO/IEC 14443 protocols. This mode is widely used in applications such as contactless payments, access control, and mobile wallets. This mode has two variants of implementation. It can either be implemented on a general-purpose MCU or using a dedicated secure element (SE) hardware, depending on the use case. The majority of security keys supporting NFC uses the SE variant (as implied, for example, by the Yubikey tech manual~\cite[yubikey-manual] or by the Google Titan documentation~\cite[titan-key-documentation]).

* {\sbf Peer-to-Peer Mode} 
\nl The peer-to-peer mode enables half-duplex communication between two NFC-enabled devices, where both devices can alternately assume the roles of initiator and target. Communication in this mode is defined by ISO/IEC 18092 and the NFC Forum Logical Link Control Protocol (LLCP). It is primarily used for data exchange scenarios such as device pairing or file transfer, although its practical use has declined in favor of higher-throughput wireless technologies.

\enditems

These operating modes build upon the previously described concepts of initiator and target roles, as well as active and passive communication modes. Their combination defines the functional behavior of NFC devices in real-world applications.

\sec[nfc-tag-types] Types of NFC Tags
\secc[nfc-a] NFC-A

{\sbf NFC-A} is based on ISO/IEC 14443 Type A. Initialization and anticollision operate at a fixed 106 kbit/s. After successful anticollision, data exchange speeds range from 106 kbit/s (fc/128) up to 27.12 Mbit/s (2fc).

\nl {\sbf Encoding and Modulation:} NFC-A employs {\sbf Miller (delay) encoding}, a line code where logical bits are represented by time-dependent transitions in signal amplitude. This encoding improves robustness against noise and enables reliable clock synchronization between reader and card.
The encoded signal then modulates the RF carrier using different schemes depending on transmission speed:
\begitems

* {\sbf 106 kbit/s to fc/16}: Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) modulation, where the carrier amplitude shifts between minimum and maximum to represent encoded bits.
* {\sbf fc/8 to fc/2}: Same modulation as NFC-B (see below).
* {\sbf 3fc/4 to 2fc}: Phase Shift Keying (PSK) modulation, where the carrier phase shifts to encode information, reducing sensitivity to amplitude noise at higher speeds.
\enditems

\secc[nfc-b] NFC-B

{\sbf NFC-B} is based on ISO/IEC 14443 Type B. Initialization and anticollision operate at 106 kbit/s, with a maximum data exchange speed of 6.78 Mbit/s (fc/2), lower than NFC-A's maximum.
{\sbf Encoding and Modulation:} NFC-B uses {\sbf NRZ-L (Non-Return-to-Zero Level)} encoding, where high and low amplitude levels directly represent logical 1 and 0 without returning to zero between bits.
Transmission is {\sbf asymmetric}:
\begitems

* {\sbf Reader-to-card}: 10\% Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) modulation, where the carrier amplitude drops to approximately 90\% to represent a logical 0, and remains at full amplitude for logical 1. The low modulation index keeps the carrier stable, reducing RF noise pickup.
* {\sbf Card-to-reader}: Binary Phase Shift Keying (BPSK) modulation of a subcarrier, which allows the card to respond without being overwhelmed by the reader's transmitted signal. The initial phase of the subcarrier represents logical 1 and a 180 degree shift represents a logical 0.
\enditems

The asymmetric modulation scheme and lower modulation index result in {\sbf improved robustness against electromagnetic interference}-a critical advantage for applications requiring high reliability.

\secc[nfc-f] NFC-F

{\sbf NFC-F} is based on the JIS X 6319-4 specification and was originally developed by Sony under the commercial name FeliCa. Unlike NFC-A and NFC-B, NFC-F is not strictly derived from the ISO/IEC 14443 family, but instead follows a different protocol structure defined in Japanese industrial standards.

\nl A key distinguishing feature of NFC-F is its higher data rates, typically 212 kbit/s or 424 kbit/s, which makes it suitable for latency-sensitive applications. The technology is used primarily in Japan, particularly in transportation systems, contactless payment infrastructures, and access control solutions.

\nl However, due to its regional focus and protocol differences, NFC-F is not universally supported by all NFC readers. Consequently, its applicability in general-purpose NFC implementations is limited and it is mentioned in this overview purely for completeness.

\sec[nfc-a-state-machine] NFC-A State Machine

NFC-A anticollision and activation sequence has a defined state machine behavior. This state machine must be respected for the correct initialization of the communication and protocol flow. 

\nl PCD can use the following commands during the anticollision sequence an NFC-A PICC:

\begitems 
* {\sbf REQA} (REQuest, Type A) 
\nl Broadcast command used to poll for any NFC-A cards in the field. Only cards in IDLE state respond.
* {\sbf WUPA} (Wake-UP, Type A) 
\nl Similar to REQA, but also wakes cards in HALT state. Cards in both IDLE and HALT states respond.
* {\sbf ANTICOLLISION}
\nl Used to resolve collisions when multiple cards respond simultaneously, by iteratively isolating individual UIDs.
* {\sbf SELECT} 
\nl Selects a specific card by its full UID, moving it into the ACTIVE (or ACTIVE$^*$) state.
* {\sbf HLTA} 
\nl Instructs the active card to enter the HALT state, suspending it until a WUPA command is received.
\enditems

The NFC-A state machine has the following States:

\begitems

* POWER-OFF 
\nl The PICC is not in an RF field exceeding Hmin. Communication is not possible. The transition to IDLE occurs when the RF field strength exceeds Hmin.

* IDLE 
\nl The PICC is powered by the RF field but has not yet been addressed. It responds only to REQA or WUPA commands. A valid REQA or WUPA transitions the card to READY.

* READY 
\nl The PICC has responded to REQA or WUPA. The anticollision loop begins here: the PCD issues ANTICOLLISION commands to isolate the card's UID, then a SELECT command to uniquely address it. A successful SELECT transitions the card to ACTIVE. A failed SELECT or collision returns the card to IDLE.

* READY$^*$ 
\nl Functionally identical to READY, but entered exclusively from HALT via WUPA. The distinction ensures that the PCD can track whether a card was freshly powered or previously halted. A successful SELECT transitions the card to ACTIVE$^*$.

* ACTIVE 
\nl The PICC has been selected by its full UID and is ready to communicate. If the card supports ISO-DEP~\ref[nfc-iso-dep], it awaits a RATS command to enter the PROTOCOL state. Otherwise, it continues with its proprietary protocol. An HLTA command transitions the card to HALT.

* ACTIVE$^*$
\nl Functionally identical to ACTIVE, but entered from READY$^*$. The distinction allows the PCD to identify cards that were reactivated from HALT, which may be relevant for session management. A HLTA command returns the card to HALT.

* HALT 
\nl The PICC is suspended and ignores all commands except WUPA. This allows the PCD to selectively wake specific cards in a multi-card scenario. A valid WUPA transitions the card to READY$^*$.

* PROTOCOL 
\nl The PICC operates under the ISO-DEP protocol, initiated by a successful RATS/ATS exchange. All subsequent communication follows the negotiated parameters (FWT, FSC, etc.). The card remains in this state until the RF field is removed or a protocol-level untick is issued, returning it to HALT or IDLE, respectively.

\enditems

\secc[nfc-a-anticollision] NFC-A Initialization and Anticollision

NFC-A uses a {\sbf bit-frame anticollision} method based on binary tree traversal. Since multiple PICCs may respond simultaneously to a REQA or WUPA command, their signals can overlap and corrupt each other, this is called a {\sbf collision}. The anticollision loop resolves collisions iteratively by narrowing down the UID of each card in the field. This overview focuses solely on the NFC-A procedures, although the standard also specifies behavior for hybrid tags supporting both the NFC-A and NFC-B types.

\seccc[nfc-a-polling] Polling

Before sending a REQA command, the PCD must present an unmodulated RF field for a minimum of {\sbf 5.1 ms}. This ensures passive PICCs, which rely on energy harvesting, are sufficiently powered before they are expected to respond. The PCD then broadcasts REQA to poll all Type A PICCs in IDLE state, or WUPA to additionally wake PICCs in HALT state.
\seccc[nfc-a-atqa] Answer To reQuest (ATQA)
Upon receiving a valid REQA or WUPA, all eligible PICCs respond simultaneously with an {\sbf ATQA (Answer To reQuest, Type A)}, a 2-byte frame encoding:
\begitems

* {\sbf Bit frame anticollision indicator} - which bit position is used as the anticollision entry point in the binary tree traversal
* {\sbf UID size indicator} - whether the UID is 4, 7, or 10 bytes, determining how many cascade levels the anticollision loop must traverse
\enditems

If multiple PICCs respond in the same slot, their ATQA frames collide at the bit level. The PCD detects this and proceeds with the anticollision loop to isolate individual cards.

\seccc[nfc-a-uid] UID Structure and Cascade Levels
NFC-A PICCs have UIDs of three possible lengths, each requiring a different number of cascade levels to fully resolve:
\begitems

* {\sbf Single (4 bytes)} - resolved in one cascade level (CL1)
* {\sbf Double (7 bytes)} - resolved across two cascade levels (CL1, CL2)
* {\sbf Triple (10 bytes)} - resolved across three cascade levels (CL1, CL2, CL3)
\enditems

Each cascade level transmits 4 bytes of UID data at a time. When more levels remain, the first byte of the current level is set to the {\sbf Cascade Tag (CT, value 88h)}, signalling to the PCD that the UID continues in a subsequent cascade level.

\seccc[nfc-a-loop] Anticollision Loop

The following sequence repeats for each required cascade level:

\begitems

* The PCD sends an {\sbf ANTICOLLISION} command specifying the current cascade level (CL1, CL2, or CL3) and an {\sbf NVB (Number of Valid Bits)} field indicating how many leading bits of the UID are already known. Initially NVB = 0, meaning no bits are known yet.

* All PICCs in READY (or READY$^*$) state whose UID matches the known prefix respond with their remaining UID bits.

* If exactly one PICC responds, no collision occurred and the PCD has resolved the full UID fragment for this level.

* If multiple PICCs respond simultaneously, a collision is detected at the first bit position where responses differ. The PCD appends either a {\sbf 0} or {\sbf 1} at the collision point, increments NVB, and reissues the ANTICOLLISION command - effectively traversing one branch of a binary tree. This repeats until a single PICC is isolated.

* Once the full 4-byte UID fragment for the current level is known, the PCD issues a {\sbf SELECT} command containing the complete fragment and a {\sbf BCC (Block Check Character)}, which is the XOR of the four UID bytes, used for integrity verification.

* The addressed PICC responds with a {\sbf SAK (Select AcKnowledge)}, confirming selection. The SAK also indicates whether further cascade levels are required (bit 2 of SAK set) or whether the full UID has been resolved (bit 2 cleared).

\enditems

Once all cascade levels are resolved and the final SAK confirms a complete UID, the PICC transitions to {\sbf ACTIVE} state. If the SAK additionally indicates ISO-DEP support (bit 5 set), the PCD may proceed with a RATS command to initiate the ISO 14443-4 protocol. Otherwise, the PICC continues with its own proprietary protocol.

\sec[nfc-protocols] Communication Protocols

NFC communication is built upon a layered protocol stack that defines how data is exchanged between devices after the initial activation phase. At the transport and higher communication layers, two primary protocols are used: ISO-DEP and NFC-DEP. In addition, several auxiliary protocol layers defined by the NFC Forum provide standardized data exchange formats.

\secc[nfc-iso-dep] ISO-DEP

The {\sbf ISO-DEP} (ISO Data Exchange Protocol), specified in ISO/IEC~14443-4~\cite[iso14443-4], defines a transport layer for reliable data exchange between a PCD and a PICC. It extends the lower NFC-A/B protocols by introducing a block-oriented, half-duplex communication mechanism that enables structured and robust transmission of application data. This is the most significant aspect of the NFC technology for the subject of this thesis.

\seccc[nfc-iso-dep-activation] Activation and Parameter Negotiation

Before data exchange can begin, ISO-DEP requires an activation phase in which the initiator selects the target and negotiates communication parameters. This is performed using the RATS (Request for Answer To Select) and ATS (Answer To Select) mechanism.

\nl First, the reader sends a RATS (Request for Answer to Select) command. RATS is a 2-byte command consisting of a fixed command byte (E0) and a parameter proposal byte (PP).

\nl The command byte E0 is fixed; its upper 4 bits encode Frame Size for proximity coupling Device Integer (FSDI, 0-15). its lower 4 bits encode the Card Identifier (CID, 0–14), which logically addresses a specific card when multiple cards are in the field. This value remains assigned to the PICC until it leaves the field. CID=15 is reserved.

The interface bytes can contain these values: 

\begitems
* {\sbf Frame Waiting Time (FWT)} 
\nl  Specifies the maximum time the initiator waits for a response from the target. It is derived from the Frame Waiting Time Integer (FWI) in TA and directly influences the allowable processing time of the card. Violating this constraint typically results in termination of the NFC session.
* {\sbf Waiting Time Extension (WTX)} 
\nl Allows the target to request additional processing time beyond the configured FWT via a special block. This mechanism is essential for operations involving computationally intensive tasks, such as cryptographic processing.
* {\sbf Frame Size (FSC/FSD)}
\nl Defined by the FSCI field in T0, it specifies the maximum payload size of a single frame for the PICC (FSC) and the PCD (FSD), determining whether chaining is required.
* {\sbf Frame Guard Time (FGT)} 
\nl Derived from the Guard Time Integer (GTI) in TB, FGT specifies the {\sbf minimum delay the target must observe between receiving a frame and sending its response}. This prevents receiver saturation and ensures reliable synchronization.
* {\sbf Device Identifier (DID)}
\nl When multiple cards are in the field, the initiator can use DID (0–14) to address a specific card. If not used, DID is typically set to 0 or omitted entirely.
* {\sbf Historical Bytes (HB)}
\nl Optional data included in the ATS, they are to be interpreted according to the card manufacturer's specifications , they may provide additional information about the capabilities of the card if present.
\enditems

Correct configuration and handling of these parameters is essential to ensure compatibility with readers and mobile devices. Strict adherence to timing constraints, such as the frame waiting time, is required to prevent premature session termination.

\seccc[nfc-iso-dep-data] Data Exchange

Communication in {\sbf ISO-DEP} is organized into protocol data units (blocks), which are exchanged between the initiator and the target. Three types of blocks are defined: {\sbf I-Blocks} to carry application data, {\sbf R-Blocks} for acknowledgments and retransmission requests, and {\sbf S-Blocks} for control operations such as waiting time extension or protocol deactivation. This structure ensures reliable communication even in the presence of transmission errors.

\seccc[nfc-apdu] APDU

ISO-DEP serves as the transport layer for {\sbf Application Protocol Data Units (APDUs)}, which implement a command–response communication model. APDUs are a standardized data format used in smart card systems for structured information exchange defined by ISO/IEC 7816-4.

\nl In this model, the initiator sends a {\sbf command APDU (C-APDU)}, and the target responds with a {\sbf response APDU (R-APDU)}. This abstraction allows higher-level protocols, including smart card applications and CTAP over NFC, to operate independently of the underlying transmission mechanism while relying on ISO-DEP for reliable delivery.

\nl A command APDU (C-APDU) consists of a header (CLA, INS, P1, P2) and an optional payload defined by the fields Lc (length of command data) and Le (expected length of response data). The response APDU (R-APDU) may contain response data and is always terminated by a two-byte status word (SW1, SW2), indicating the result of the operation.

\nl APDUs can be encoded in short or extended format. In the short format, the length fields are limited to one byte, allowing payloads of up to 255 bytes. The extended format uses two-byte length fields, enabling payloads of up to 65,535 bytes.

\nl In the short format, the C-APDU consists of the mentioned 4-byte header followed by the optional fields Lc, Data (0–255 bytes) and Le, where both Lc and Le are encoded using a single byte.

\seccc[nfc-chaining-difference] Chaining mechanisms

Within ISO-DEP, APDUs are transported using I-Blocks. Since the maximum size of a single ISO-DEP frame is limited by the negotiated frame size, APDUs exceeding this size must be fragmented. This is achieved through {\sbf ISO-DEP chaining}, where a single APDU is split across multiple I-Blocks and reassembled by the receiving side.

\nl In addition to transport-level fragmentation, large responses may also be handled at the application level. In the case of short APDUs, the response length is limited by the value of the Le field. If the response exceeds this limit, it can be delivered in multiple steps using the status word mechanism (e.g., SW1~=~\code{0x61}), where the client issues the GET RESPONSE command to retrieve the remaining data.

\nl The two chaining mechanisms operate at different layers: ISO-DEP chaining ensures reliable transmission of a single APDU over multiple frames, while application-level response handling enables the transfer of data exceeding the limits of the APDU format itself.

\secc[nfc-nfc-dep] NFC-DEP

The {\sbf NFC-DEP} (NFC Data Exchange Protocol), defined in ISO/IEC~18092, is designed for peer-to-peer communication between two {\sbf active} NFC devices. Unlike ISO-DEP, which follows a reader–card model, NFC-DEP enables symmetric communication, allowing both devices to alternate between initiator and target roles.

\nl In contrast to ISO-DEP, {\sbf NFC-DEP does not utilize APDUs} and does not follow a strict command–response model. Instead, communication is based on {\sbf message-oriented data exchange}, making it more suitable for peer-to-peer scenarios rather than smart card emulation.

\nl For this reason, NFC-DEP is {\sbf not applicable} to the desired goal of this thesis, as FIDO2 authentication over NFC relies on APDU-based communication provided by ISO-DEP.
